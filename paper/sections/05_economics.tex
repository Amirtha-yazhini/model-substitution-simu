\section{Economics and the Routing Confound}\label{sec:economics}
\textbf{Break-even.} At dilution rate $\epsilon$ the gateway saves
$\$1{,}000 \cdot \epsilon \cdot (1 - r)$ per \$1,000 of billed traffic, where $r = 0.115$ is the
price ratio of substitute to genuine model. We compare this with the imputed cost of enough audit
sessions to reach 80\% detection power, using measured token counts and public list prices
(Fig.~\ref{fig:F2}). Cost is never the obstacle. One audit session costs between \$0.0013 (KBF) and
\$0.014 (FUSE), and wherever an auditor reaches 80\% power the total is below one cent, against
savings of tens of dollars. On price alone, auditing always pays. The obstacle is false positives.
Repeating a session raises power, but it also raises false alarms: with per-session false-positive
rate $f$, $k$ sessions produce at least one false alarm with probability $1 - (1-f)^k$. The protocol
seals a family-wise budget of 5\%, which limits each auditor to between 6 and 15 sessions. Below
$\epsilon = 0.10$ per-session power collapses. At $\epsilon = 0.07$ the best auditor detects 6\% of
sessions on the hand-written ladder and 1\% on the fitted one, so no admissible number of repeats
reaches 80\%. The smallest dilution an admissible audit detects is therefore $\epsilon^* = 0.10$ on
both ladders, with a bootstrap 95\% interval of [0.10, 0.10]. IRIS-lite reaches it with 34\% power
per session and 4 sessions on the hand-written ladder, or 56\% and 2 sessions on the fitted one,
for \$0.003--\$0.006.

One caveat moves this number. IRIS-lite sets $\epsilon^*$ on both ladders, but on real recordings
its features cannot separate 20b from 120b (Section~\ref{sec:real}), so its signal on the mock
comes from hand-written surface habits. Removing it in a post-hoc, unsealed check leaves
$\epsilon^* = 0.10$ on the hand-written ladder and raises it to $0.15$ on the fitted ladder, in both
cases set by GATEOPS. We therefore claim $\epsilon^* \in [0.10, 0.15]$. A rational gateway sets
$\epsilon$ just below this range. At $\epsilon = 0.05$ it still saves \$44 per \$1,000, and no
auditor here detects it within a false-alarm budget a customer would accept.

\textbf{The confound.} A3 at $\epsilon = 0.10$ sends 10\% of traffic to a cheaper model. A11
spreads traffic across honest providers of the correct model. The first is fraud; the second is
ordinary load balancing, which routers do by default. Fig.~\ref{fig:F6} and
Table~\ref{tab:confound} compare them over 200 sessions each.

GATEOPS finds benign routing \emph{more} anomalous than fraud (AUROC 0.00--0.03), and FUSE inherits
the problem: on the fitted ladder it flags half of all honest routing sessions and no fraud
sessions. Timing and metadata measure whether traffic is \emph{heterogeneous}, not whether the
model is \emph{wrong}, and honest routing is as heterogeneous as traffic gets. The text auditors
separate the two arms only because our simulated alternate provider returns nearly identical text.
A real alternate provider with a different inference engine or quantisation would shift the text
as well, and the gap would narrow.

We read this as evidence that ``model substitution'' covers three violations, each needing
different evidence:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Identity:} the weights are wrong (A1, A2, A7). Content signals can observe this.
\item \textbf{Disclosure:} routing happens but is not disclosed (A3 vs.\ A11). The two arms differ
  only in intent, which no client-side measurement observes.
\item \textbf{Billing:} the invoice does not match the service (A8). Checking this needs an
  invoice, and free tiers issue none.
\end{itemize}
Only identity is a detection problem for black-box auditors. Disclosure needs an obligation on the
gateway to report its routing, and billing needs verifiable metering. This arrives at the
recommendation of hardware attestation in~\cite{cai2025} from the other direction: part of what
auditors are asked to detect cannot be observed from the client at all.
